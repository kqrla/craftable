# tuftistry, explained from scratch

this document is meant to stand on its own. you do not need to have read anything else in this repo to follow it.

i'm building a machine that makes tufted rugs on demand from generated designs, and i call the technique tuftistry. it is one front of a larger project called craftable machines, which is about getting machines to make physical, handmade-looking things, but this front works as its own idea and that is how i'm explaining it here. nothing in this branch has been built yet. everything below is the plan, and the images in the hardware folder are ai-generated concept sketches of what the finished thing could look like, not photos of a real build.

tufting is how most handmade modern rugs get made. a piece of backing cloth is stretched tight across a frame, the design is drawn or projected onto it, and a person traces the design with a tufting gun, which punches yarn through the cloth in loops. you work region by region, filling each color area with pile. when the whole design is filled, the cloth comes off the frame and gets finished into a rug. it is slow, physical work, and the person doing the tracing is most of the labor cost.

the machine replaces that tracing step. the frame stands upright, a projector maps the design onto the cloth so the layout lands correctly on the real surface, and a motorized arm holds the tufting gun and follows the design region by region. the word "mapping" matters here. the design has to be warped to match where the frame actually sits in the room, so that what the arm traces lines up with what is on the cloth. a planning program, which i call the engine, reads the design image, finds each flat color region, puts the regions in an efficient order, and writes out the paths the arm should follow, along with settings like needle depth, loop height and yarn tension. the arm just executes. there is no paint, nothing wet, nothing that behaves unpredictably over time, which is the main reason tufting is a much easier problem than painting would be.

the designs come from a small generative program written in p5.js, a javascript drawing library that runs in a browser. each time you refresh the page it produces a new composition made of flat color regions with hard black borders, using a fixed palette, so every piece is unique but the collection still looks like it belongs together. the current example sketch builds its compositions from randomly placed seed points that grow into organic cells with wobbly ink-like edges, a couple of which get concentric rings, and it picks one of four palettes per run. that sketch is only an example. the engine does not care where a design comes from, only that it has flat regions, hard borders and a bounded palette, so the source can be swapped for any other program, or a hand-drawn design, that meets that description.

this is deliberately not ai art. the design program is ordinary deterministic code, not a trained model. that means there is no training data and so no question about whose artwork it learned from, there is no inference cost because it is one file that runs in a browser and gives a new design instantly, and there is no model to fine-tune or maintain. i think that is a real advantage over pipelines that need a model, a gpu and a story about where the training data came from.

the reason to do rugs specifically is that this is one of the few places where a robot's output holds its value. a painting is priced largely on who made it, and a machine has no name that sells. a rug is priced on its material and the visible labor in it, so a machine-tufted rug can carry the same perceived value as a hand-tufted one. the closest company i know of is arcade.ai, which raised a 25 million dollar series a for custom rugs designed with ai and made by human artisans. that shows people will pay for custom rugs, but their cost and speed are capped by the artisans. replacing the artisan with an arm that follows instructions directly should lower the cost per rug and let throughput grow, and the on-demand model means nothing gets made until someone has actually ordered it.

to be clear about what is not new: robotic tufting itself already exists. there are commercial robotic tufting machines, and hobbyists have mounted tufting guns on robot arms. i am not claiming the mechanism as an invention and it should never be pitched that way. the contribution is the whole chain around it, from a generated design, to an engine that turns it into instructions, to a machine that executes them, to an on-demand way of selling the result.

right now the plan is at the idea stage. the first things to prove are the projection mapping, so the engine's output lands correctly on a real frame, and then an arm that can hold a tufting gun and follow a single projected line without wandering. after that comes a full single region, then a full multi-region design, then a complete rug, then a sellable one. one idea i have deliberately parked for later is varying the pile height across a rug to make it sculptural, which would need a depth channel alongside the color. flat pile comes first.
